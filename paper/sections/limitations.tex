\section*{Limitations}

We study two languages, and the lexical resources differ between
them: the Portuguese lexicon links fewer pairs, and PT-LKB contains no antonyms, so antonymy comes only
from Wiktionary and ConceptNet. Results may differ for languages with poorer resources or a different
morphology, and the lexical relations are looked up without word-sense disambiguation.

Coverage treats every hypothesis word as a requirement. It cannot represent
downward-monotone contexts, restrictive modifiers or universal quantifiers, where removing or adding a
word preserves the inference, and \model{} loses to the baseline on these AX-b phenomena. The conflict
term, motivated by the label semantics, brought no measurable benefit in our experiments.

The structured branch learned on English data does not transfer to
Portuguese: in that direction \model{} is below the \cls{} baseline on two of four test sets, and no
ablation removes the gap. We do not claim that the method helps zero-shot transfer.

Training uses only SICK and InferBR, two small corpora; we have not
tested larger training sets such as MNLI. Several test sets are only binary, so out-of-distribution
results use the entailment decision, and ASSIN~2 overlaps with SICK, which we handle by removing the
overlapping pairs. The AX-b phenomenon groups are small, and their differences are exploratory.

The knowledge-graph baselines are re-implementations under our
protocol, not the original systems, and they take their knowledge from English WordNet. Hyperparameters
are fixed in advance and not tuned per encoder, which may favour some systems. Albertina and XLM-R-large
are trained with 8-bit optimizer states and gradient checkpointing to fit a 12\,GB GPU.

The per-word scores explain the structured branch exactly, but the final
prediction also includes the \cls{} logits, which are not explained. The scores show which words drive
the structured decision, not why the encoder assigns them.
